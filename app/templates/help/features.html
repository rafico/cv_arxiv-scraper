How Ranking Works

The score is a recommendation signal, not a judgment of paper quality. If something looks wrong, use Save or Not Interested and the ranking will adapt.

Papers rise to the top when they align with more of the signals you care about:

  • They match authors, labs, or topics from your Research Setup.
  • They are recent, unless your freshness settings prefer older work too.
  • They are highly cited.
  • You have previously saved similar papers.
  • AI scoring is enabled and rates the paper as relevant.

Signals stack. A paper by a tracked author at a tracked lab on a tracked topic will usually rank much higher than a paper matching only one weak signal.

Ranking Explanations

Each paper shows why it was recommended. Expand the score details when you want to understand whether a result came from an author match, a topic match, citation strength, or saved-paper similarity.

  • “Matched author: Kaiming He”
  • “Highly cited (342 citations)”
  • “Similar to saved: NeRF in the Wild”

Inline score-factor bars

Turn on Settings → Ranking → Show score factor bars on papers to see the top few contributors (e.g. Match, For you, Terms) as small colored bars right on each row or card — an at-a-glance “why this ranked” without expanding the full breakdown. The points shown are recency-adjusted, so they line up with the headline score.

Cold-Start & Active Learning

The learned interest profile needs about five saved papers before it kicks in. These two tools get you there faster on a fresh install.

Bootstrap from arXiv IDs

In Settings → Research Setup, paste a list of arXiv IDs or links and click Bootstrap interests. They are fetched, embedded, and recorded as positive signals, so the profile activates immediately. Re-running it is safe — already-saved papers stay saved.

Help ranking learn

Once you have a few saves, the dashboard sidebar shows a Help ranking learn widget with a couple of borderline papers — the ones the model is most unsure about. Saving or skipping them is the most informative feedback you can give.

Enrichment

Enrichment means the app adds extra metadata after a paper is ingested, so you can sort, filter, and inspect papers with more context.

TL;DR Summaries

Every paper card shows a short summary below the authors. By default this is an extractive first sentence from the abstract. When LLM is enabled, it becomes a plain-language AI-generated TL;DR describing what the paper does and why it matters. You can control how many lines are shown via Settings → Ranking → Summary lines.

Citations

Citation counts from Semantic Scholar. Sort by Most Cited when you want influential work first instead of the newest work.

OpenAlex

Topic classifications, open-access status, and additional citation data from OpenAlex. Enabled by default so papers arrive with better metadata.

SPECTER2 Embeddings

Scientific paper embeddings power semantic search and related-paper recommendations. This is what lets the app find papers by meaning, not just exact words.

PDF Thumbnails

First-page thumbnails for quick visual scanning. Helpful when you want to skim many papers faster.

Corpus Analytics

Use these tools when you want to explore your whole library at a higher level instead of reviewing one paper at a time.

Topic Clusters

Groups recent papers by theme so you can see which areas of your feed are active.

Emerging Topics

Compares recent trends against a baseline to spot research directions that are starting to pick up.

Neighbor Papers

Finds additional papers similar to a collection or a set of seed papers you already like.

Citation Graph

The Graph page (sidebar) draws your library as a force-directed network: an arrow from one paper to another means the first cites the second, using reference lists from OpenAlex. Node color runs light→dark by publication year, node size reflects PageRank within your corpus — the papers your library keeps citing float to the top visually. Filter by collection or year range, hide papers with no local citations, and double-click a node to open that paper on the dashboard.

Edges only connect papers you actually track and are refreshed automatically after every scrape. For papers added before this feature existed, run cv-arxiv-backfill citation-edges once to fetch their reference lists.

Ask This Paper

Expand a paper’s details and open Ask this paper to ask questions answered only from that paper’s own indexed text. Each claim is cited as [1], [2]… — click a citation chip to see the quoted passage and which section it came from. Citations that don’t map to a real excerpt are stripped automatically, and when the paper doesn’t cover your question the answer says so instead of guessing. Follow-up questions in the same panel keep the conversation context.

Answers need an LLM enabled; without one you get the paper’s most relevant sections instead. Papers scraped before full-text section extraction answer from the abstract only (the panel notes this).

Full-text sections are now read from arXiv’s clean HTML rendition when it exists (better structure, literal code links, and math), falling back to the PDF for older papers. Re-run extraction over your library with cv-arxiv-backfill sections (add --force to upgrade papers whose sections came from the PDF).

Citation Verification

Every AI answer — in Ask this paper and in Chat with your saved papers — is checked against your own library. Any arXiv ID, DOI, or paper title the answer names is resolved locally:

  • A ✓ verified reference is one that exists in your corpus — click the green chip to open your copy.
  • An ⚠ unverified reference is not in your library and may be fabricated. Treat it with suspicion until you confirm it independently.

A short “N of M references verified” note summarizes each answer. This runs on every answer automatically — there is no setting to turn it off, because it is a guarantee rather than an option.

Related Papers & Duplicates

Related Papers

Expand a paper’s details to see similar papers already in your library. This is useful when you want alternative baselines, follow-up work, or adjacent methods.

Duplicate Detection

Papers with nearly identical titles get an orange Dup badge so you can avoid reviewing or exporting the same work twice.

Implementation Readiness

Papers whose code looks ready to run get a ⚙ Runnable badge. The score combines signals already collected during enrichment — a GitHub repo, its star count, a recognised (ideally permissive) license, whether the repo is archived, how recently it was pushed, plus a project page or Hugging Face upvotes. Hover the badge to see which signals earned it.

Pick Runnable (has code) in the sidebar’s resource filter to show only these papers, and it adds a small Runnable factor to a paper’s score bars. The boost is intentionally tiny (tune readiness_weight in Ranking) so it never outweighs what you actually save and skip.
